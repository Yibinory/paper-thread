# Research a concept, not just its use in a paper

Read this when creating or substantively rewriting a knowledge page. The aim is an independently sourced tutorial. The current paper may show an application but cannot stand in for the work that introduced the idea.

## Establish the source lineage

Search using canonical names, alternative Chinese/English names, definitions, cited author/year and related historical terminology. Follow the triggering paper's bibliography, then check the cited work's own attribution. Distinguish these roles:

- **Origin/foundational work:** introduces the object, formulation, algorithm or result. Read the relevant full-text sections and verify authors, date and version.
- **Authoritative exposition:** explains accepted definitions, proofs and conditions; an author-hosted textbook or research monograph can fill teaching gaps in a terse original.
- **Variant or application work:** primary evidence for the particular modification or application being explained.
- **Triggering paper:** evidence only for how that paper uses the concept, unless it actually introduces it.

These roles may overlap; record the reason. A tutorial or survey is useful for locating originals but is not proof that its favored citation was the first. Do not call a modern application the origin of an older mathematical object. For distributed origins, name the relevant contributions and distinguish first publication, later translation and modern notation. Do not force an inventor/date when historical priority is uncertain.

Actively browse for new or substantially rewritten content even if an earlier page has citations. Reading an abstract, search result or another paper's paraphrase is not reading the source. Prefer author pages, publisher/conference repositories and scholarly archives. Search snippets are discovery evidence only. Inspect PDF images for symbols that extraction may corrupt. Do not search private draft text or upload it externally.

If the original cannot be obtained, try a lawful author copy, accepted manuscript, translation or archival edition. Then state which source actually supports the explanation and the limited confidence in attribution. Do not fabricate access, quote unseen passages or stop explaining an established concept solely because an ancient original is inaccessible. A narrow factual edit may reuse already verified evidence if its exact scope and source version still match.

## Build a source-to-explanation map

Before writing, list the tutorial's necessary learning units: motivating problem, definitions, central results/derivations, algorithm, examples, applications, variants and limitations. Add units discovered while reading; the triggering report's needs do not cap this map. For each unit record:

`unit → source ID/role/version → exact section/equation/page → what it supports → independent check needed → tutorial section`.

Separate what a source claims from what you establish. Mark author-created examples and derive their results yourself. Any empirical number needs its experiment setting and location; a toy calculation is not a benchmark. Do not transfer one variant's theorem to the whole concept or translate an asymptotic claim into a finite-sample guarantee.

Place source links beside the definition, theorem, algorithm or historical claim they support. Cite the variant's actual primary paper when explaining its changes. At the end, a short reading guide can explain which source supplies foundations, computation or an application; a source dump does not replace adjacent evidence. Record URLs, accessed version/date, inspected passages and access limits in the audit. Link only sources actually checked for the claim, and write the tutorial in original explanatory prose rather than reproducing large copyrighted passages.
